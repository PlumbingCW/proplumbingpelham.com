Sewer Camera Inspection in Pelham, AL

A sewer camera inspection shows what is inside the pipe between your house and the public sewer. This page covers when to book one, what the footage means, and what a camera cannot see.

A sewer inspection camera run is a short, planned visit in which a plumber sends a waterproof camera through your sewer line to see what is inside. Sewer camera inspection, also called sewer video inspection, can show roots, cracks, sags, offset joints and buildup that a cleaning never reveals. Same-day appointments are available when the schedule allows, and the visit suits homeowners, buyers and landlords in Pelham, AL.

What a camera shows that cleaning never will

A cable or a jetting hose works blind. It clears a path and tells you only that water now flows. A camera shows why the pipe filled. A plumber can see a root mass pushing through a joint, a wipe collar that formed at a ledge, a sag holding water, or a joint where two sections no longer line up.

The camera head carries a light and a lens on a flexible rod, and a locator at the surface can mark its depth and position. That is how a plumber turns a footage time stamp into a spot you can point to in the yard.

Moments when a camera run pays off

  • Before buying a house, so the line is known before the sale closes.
  • After two or more stoppages in the same line, to find the cause of the repeat.
  • Before a jetting job on an old or doubtful pipe, as explained under hydro jetting.
  • After a sewage backup, to see whether the pipe is damaged.
  • Before building a deck, pool, addition or driveway over or near the line.
  • When a sewer smell, a wet patch in the yard or a sunken spot has no clear source.

Repeat clogs are the most common reason to book. If the same line has needed clearing twice, a third cleaning without footage is guesswork. Pairing the camera with the next cleaning lets the plumber look at the pipe the moment it opens, which can save a second visit.

Planned work also means you can pick the timing. A buyer with a closing date or a landlord between tenants can ask for the earliest open slot when calling, since same-day appointments are available when the schedule allows.

The visit, from cleanout to footage

  1. Find the way in

    The plumber looks for a cleanout, and if there is none, the camera may go in through a removed toilet or a vent stack.

  2. Clear if needed

    A line that is full or heavily coated may need a pass with a cable first so the lens can see.

  3. Run the camera

    The camera travels toward the public main while the plumber watches the screen and notes the distance.

  4. Locate the faults

    Each fault is noted by distance and, with a locator, can be marked at the surface.

  5. Go over the findings

    Ask the plumber to walk you through what the camera showed and what the next step would be, if any. Ask what you will be given to keep, such as a recording or notes.

Reading the footage

Most findings fall into a few types. The table shows what each looks like and what usually happens next.

Camera findings and the usual next step
FindingWhat it looks likeUsual next step
Root intrusionPale fibrous threads at jointsCutting, then a schedule or a joint repair
Offset jointPipe edges that do not line upMonitor if minor, spot repair if it traps debris
Sag or bellyStanding water in a low stretchRegrade or replace that section

Heavy buildupGrease or scale narrowing the boreJetting or cleaning
Cannot tellWater in the line, a tight bend, or a point where the camera stopsClear the line, try another way in, or use a different test

Not every finding needs action. A minor offset or a thin film of grease can be watched over time. Roots, a sag that holds water or a break are the ones that lead to repair, so ask the plumber to rank the findings by urgency and to say which can wait.

Buying a house with a sewer line you cannot see

A home inspection usually covers the visible plumbing. The buried sewer line is not visible, so a camera inspection is how you see it. A recording can give you something concrete to bring to the negotiation, whether that is a repair credit, a price change or a decision to walk away.

Before you book, ask what the plumber will give you to keep, such as the recording or notes, and ask for each problem to be located by distance from the cleanout.

Ask the seller what they know, since the camera then has something to be checked against.

  • The year the house was built and whether the sewer line was ever replaced.
  • Any past backups, and what was done about them.
  • Records of root cutting or cleanings.
  • Where the cleanout is, if there is one.

Buyers looking in Alabaster and nearby towns can book the same visit, and a call before your closing date leaves time to act on what the footage shows.

What a camera cannot do

A camera cannot see through a full pipe, so a blocked line must be partly cleared first. It cannot enter if there is no access point, and it cannot see inside walls or under slabs beyond the pipe it runs through. Tight bends and a collapsed section may stop it.

It also shows only what is in view at the moment. A hairline crack on dry pipe may not show, and a line that only backs up when the whole house runs water may look fine on a quiet day. Tell the plumber about patterns even if the footage looks clean.
