Emergency calls after closing time

Commercial water problems usually surface after the building has sat unused: overnight, over a weekend, or on a holiday. For example, a restaurant might find water in the dish area when the first cook arrives, or a shop might find a ceiling stain on Monday. Water that runs unseen for those hours does the most damage, so call before you start cleanup.

Tell the dispatcher what kind of building it is, how many floors, whether the water is clean or sewage, and where the main shutoff is. If you do not know where it is, find it before an emergency. In multi-tenant buildings, the shutoff may be in a utility room or in a locked mechanical space. If you cannot find it, the dispatcher can talk you through the search.

Have ready when you call

  • The building type, such as restaurant, retail suite, or office
  • Whether the water is clean or looks like sewage
  • Where the main shutoff is, or that you cannot find it

Who pays: tenant, landlord, and what to document

Leases decide who is responsible for what, and the answer differs between buildings. A problem inside your suite, such as a fixture, a faucet, or a line from your floor drain, can fall to the tenant. A problem in a shared riser, a roof drain, or the main sewer line can fall to the owner, but your lease and the property manager settle it.

Read your lease, but do not wait for an answer before you protect the space. Take photos, note the time, and write down what the plumber finds. Keep the invoice. If the cause turns out to be the building and not your suite, that record is what you show the property manager.

Grease traps and interceptors

A grease trap catches fats, oils, and food solids before they reach the sewer. When it fills, it stops working, and grease moves down the line and hardens. Signs are slow kitchen drains, foul odor near the dish area, and repeated clogs that clear and return.

Cleaning means pumping out the contents, scraping the walls, and checking the baffles and seals. Small units under the sink can often be cleaned by hand, while large in-ground interceptors are usually a bigger job. Ask the plumber what the visit includes. Keep a dated record of each cleaning and who did it. Pelham Water Works handles sewer, so ask them whether any local requirements apply.

Backflow testing

A backflow preventer is a valve assembly that stops used water from flowing back into the public supply. Irrigation systems, fire lines, boilers, and carwash equipment are typical places for them. They have moving parts that can wear, so they are worth having checked.

Testing rules for backflow devices can come from the water utility, the state, or the plumbing code, and they depend on the device and how it is used. Ask Pelham Water Works at (205) 620-6420 or the Pelham Building Department at 205.620.6495 which rules apply to your building. A plumber can test the device, report the result, and repair or replace it if it fails. Keep the report on file.

Alabama's Plumbers and Gas Fitters Examining Board rule 720-X-1-.02 names the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC) for commercial plumbing, so check with the board for the current edition. You can also verify any plumber's license in the board's Licensee Search at pgfb.alabama.gov.

Flushometers, floor drains, and storm drains

Commercial toilets and urinals use flushometers, valves that deliver a measured burst of water directly from the supply line. When the diaphragm wears, the valve either sticks open and runs, or fails to flush. A plumber can often rebuild the valve. One that sticks open can waste a lot of water overnight, so close its supply stop if you can reach it.

Floor drains protect kitchens, mechanical rooms, and restrooms. They dry out if unused, and then sewer smell enters the room. Pouring water in them periodically keeps the trap sealed. A drain that backs up needs drain cleaning of the line beyond it.

Outdoor storm drains and lot inlets are a separate matter from the plumbing inside your building. If water ponds in your lot after a storm, describe it to the dispatcher so you can be told whether it is a plumbing job, and ask your property manager who maintains the lot drainage.

Hidden water use and what leak detection can find

If your water usage is high and no leak is visible, leak detection can help. A plumber may use listening gear and pressure testing to narrow the search. It will not always find the leak, and in a large building it may take more than one visit.

Planned work can happen before opening, after closing, or on a day you are closed. Tell the dispatcher your hours, whether the building is shared, and who can let the plumber in. In a shared building, ask the property manager to warn other tenants before the water goes off.

For new fixtures, restroom additions, or buildouts, plumbing installation covers the rough-in and finish. Permit rules vary by job, so ask the Pelham Building Department at 205.620.6495, which issues permits and inspects commercial and residential construction inside city limits.
