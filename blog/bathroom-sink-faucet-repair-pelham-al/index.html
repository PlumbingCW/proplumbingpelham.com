Bathroom Sink Faucet Repair in Pelham, AL

Most bathroom faucet problems come down to one worn part. Find which, see what the fix involves, and decide whether repair or replacement makes more sense.

By the Pelham Pro Plumbing team. Published October 15, 2026. Updated October 15, 2026.

A bathroom faucet fails in a handful of predictable ways. It drips from the spout, leaks around the handle, sprays sideways, or runs weak. Each of those points to a different small part, and most can be handled with a screwdriver, an adjustable wrench, and some patience. The first job is to work out which kind of faucet you have, because that decides the fix.

Work out which faucet you own

Look at the handles. A single lever or one round knob that moves in several directions is usually a cartridge or ball style faucet. Two separate handles generally mean either compression stems, which close against a rubber washer, or two ceramic disc cartridges that turn a quarter or half turn.

If the handle keeps turning and feels firmer the harder you push, it is probably a compression faucet with a washer. If it turns smoothly with no resistance and stops abruptly, it is likely a ceramic disc cartridge. Photograph the faucet before you start, since brand names are often hidden under a cap.

Prepare so the repair stays small

Close both oval stop valves under the sink by turning them clockwise until snug. Open the faucet to drain the lines. Stop the drain with a rag or the pop-up, lay a towel in the basin, and set aside a small cup for screws.

If a stop valve will not turn or drips when you close it, do not force it. An old stop that cracks can flood the cabinet. In that case, shut the whole house off at the main and let a plumber replace the stop along with the repair.

Fixing a drip at the spout

A steady drip means water is passing a worn seal. On a compression faucet the culprit is the rubber washer or the brass seat it presses against. On a cartridge faucet it is the cartridge itself or the rubber seals around it.

Pry off the decorative cap, remove the handle screw, and lift the handle away. Unscrew the stem or pull the cartridge, noting its orientation. Replace the washer and seat, or the whole cartridge. A worn seat can be dressed with a seat tool, but a pitted one is easier to replace than to keep resurfacing. Note the faucet's model, the cartridge shape and the number of splines, or take photos, before buying a replacement.

Leaks around the handle or base

Water seeping beneath the handle when the tap is on is a sign of a dry or cut O-ring on the stem or cartridge. Rolling a new O-ring on, with a thin coat of plumber's grease, usually stops it. Check also that the packing nut at the base is snug, not cranked down.

If water collects at the base of the spout, the O-rings in the swivel joint are suspect, which is common on a faucet with a swivel spout. Remove the spout, replace the rings, and lightly grease them before it goes back on.

Low flow and spray problems

If the faucet runs weakly, first unscrew the aerator at the tip of the spout. It holds a small screen that catches grit and mineral flakes, and it clogs more often than anything else. Soak the screen in white vinegar for an hour, brush it clean, and reinstall.

If weak flow persists after the aerator is clean, check the flexible supply lines and the stops for kinks or partial closures. If only the hot side is weak, a sediment flake may have lodged in the cartridge, so remove and rinse it.

If the same faucet keeps needing repairs, replacing it often costs less trouble than another round of parts. A plumber doing plumbing installation can fit a new faucet, supply lines, and stops together so the entire connection is renewed, and a visit for faucet repair can help you decide which side of that line you are on.

Supply lines and stops while you are under there

Once the cabinet is open, look at the two braided supply lines running from the stops to the faucet. Press a dry paper towel along each connection. A damp towel means a weeping nut or a worn washer, and tightening a quarter turn often fixes it. Braided lines that are kinked, rusty at the nut, or bulging should simply be replaced.

Putting it back together and testing

  1. Reassemble in reverse

    Refit the cartridge or stem in the same orientation, then the handle, screw and cap. Do not over-tighten.

  2. Turn the stops on slowly

    Open each stop a little at a time so the lines fill without a surge.

  3. Flush with the aerator off

    Let water run for a minute with the aerator removed to clear debris, then reinstall the aerator.

  4. Check every connection

    Press a dry paper towel on each joint under the sink and look again after ten minutes. If any joint is damp, shut the stops and recheck the fit.

If a handle will not come off, do not pry hard. A little vinegar around the screw or set screw can free mineral buildup, and if it still holds, that is a good time to call a plumber. Faucet and stop work falls under the plumbing code, so check the Alabama Plumbers and Gas Fitters Examining Board for the current edition of the International Residential Code it names in rule 720-X-1-.02.

Safety and limits

Stop work and call for help if the shutoff valve will not close, if the wall is wet behind the vanity, or if the drain pipe leaks as well as the faucet. Do not try to fix a leak that runs inside the wall. Call the 24/7 line for active water you cannot stop.
