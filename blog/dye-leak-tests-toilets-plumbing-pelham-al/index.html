Dye Leak Tests for Toilets and Plumbing in Pelham, AL

Ten drops of color can tell you whether a toilet is leaking silently. This guide covers the test, the UV version, and the limits.

Call (855) 575-2890

By the Pelham Pro Plumbing team. Published October 15, 2026. Updated October 15, 2026.

A dye test uses color to show where water is going when you cannot see it. In plumbing it is most useful for toilets, where a worn flapper lets tank water slip into the bowl without a sound. This post covers the toilet test first, then the UV version for drain, overflow and shower-pan leaks.

What dye can find and what it misses

Dye works where water sits still or flows slowly and you can watch the other side. A toilet tank and bowl, a tub overflow, a shower pan, and a drain trap all fit that description. You add color on one side and look for it on the other.

It does not work on pressurized supply lines. A pinhole in a copper pipe inside a wall or under a slab sprays out under pressure, and no dye you pour into a fixture will ever reach it. For those, a plumber can use the water meter, acoustic listening gear and thermal imaging, which is what leak detection is for.

Food coloring works fine for toilets. Pick a dark color like blue or red, since pale yellow is hard to spot. Dye tablets made for toilets do the same job, so follow the package for the wait time.

UV dye glows under a black light. That lets you follow dyed drain or overflow water to the spot where it shows up, such as a ceiling stain below a bathroom. Most UV leak tracers use a fluorescent dye, so look for one labeled for plumbing or potable-safe use. Dye made for car fluids does not belong in a home water system.

The toilet test, step by step

  1. Prep the tank

    Take the lid off the tank, flush once, and let the tank refill. Remove any in-tank cleaner tablets first, since they color the water and spoil the result. Do not flush again until the test ends.

  2. Add color to the tank

    Drop in ten or so drops of food coloring or one dye tablet. Do not stir, and set the lid back.

  3. Wait without flushing

    Leave it alone for 20 to 30 minutes. Do not use that toilet during the wait, and warn the household.

  4. Look in the bowl

    Check the water in the bowl, not the tank. Any color that has reached the bowl means water has been slipping past the flapper.

  5. Flush to clear it

    Flush a few times so the dye does not stain the porcelain, and wipe up any spill.

A tank that refills itself every few minutes, or a toilet that hisses or runs briefly with nobody near it, often has this problem. The dye test confirms what you suspect.

Toilet silent but the bill is high? Turn off every fixture, then check whether the meter's low-flow dial still moves. If it does, dye the toilet first, since it is the usual cause.

Using UV dye and a black light

For a leak that is not a toilet, UV dye lets you follow the water. If a ceiling below a bathroom is damp, add UV dye to the tub or shower, run the water for a few minutes, then turn off the lights and sweep the ceiling or crawlspace with a black light. Glowing water points to that fixture as the source.

Test one fixture at a time. Dye the tub overflow, then the drain, then the shower pan, and wait between each so you can tell which one produced the glow. Wear UV-blocking glasses, since UV light can hurt eyes, and never shine it at anyone.

A plumber can run the same trace faster, because tracing several fixtures in order is easier with practice and the right tools. Many leaks inside a wall still need a camera or moisture scan after the dye narrows the area. Repairs to fixtures fall under the plumbing code, and the Alabama Plumbers and Gas Fitters Examining Board rule 720-X-1-.02 names the 2021 International Residential Code for residential work, so check the board for the current edition.

When it is time to call

Call if the dye shows a leak and a new flapper does not fix it, if the toilet base is wet, or if the bowl never fills right. Call too if you suspect a supply or drain leak and your tests keep pointing at a wall or the ceiling.

If water is on the floor near outlets or appliances, shut the toilet's supply valve (the oval or round handle low on the wall) and keep power tools and cords out of the wet area. If you cannot reach the breaker from dry footing, call the 24/7 line.

A toilet that rocks, cracks, or leaks at the base needs a plumber, because the wax seal and the flange sit below the bowl. Our toilet repair page lists what gets replaced and why. If you also suspect a pipe leak, call to book a plumber and ask about same-day appointments when available.

Homes in Alabaster and Helena have the same toilets and the same flappers, so this test applies there too. The Alabaster page and the Helena page explain how the dispatch line handles those towns.

Toilet leaks and the Pelham leak adjustment policy

Pelham Water Works has a leak adjustment policy for high bills, and it lists leaking toilets and dripping faucets among the exclusions. In plain terms, a silent toilet leak found with a dye test is not the kind of leak the city adjusts, so do not wait for a credit. Fix the flapper as soon as the dye shows color.

If a different, hidden leak did run up your bill, the policy asks for a Leak Adjustment Form and at least one proof of repair (the plumber's invoice, receipts for materials, or photos) within 60 days after the repair. The policy is meant for hidden leaks that ran usage well above your normal, so check eligibility with Pelham Water Works at (205) 620-6420 before you count on it. This is policy at time of writing, so confirm current terms.
