Replacing a Toilet Wax Ring in Pelham, AL

How to tell the wax ring has failed, what a careful DIY replacement involves, and the damage that means you should stop and bring in a plumber.

Call (855) 575-2890

Three telltale signs

Water at the base, a sewer smell, or a toilet that rocks.

Check the flange

A cracked or low flange can ruin a new seal.

Mind the subfloor

Soft or dark wood under the toilet means stop and get it assessed.

By the Pelham Pro Plumbing team. Published October 15, 2026. Updated October 15, 2026.

A wax ring is the soft donut of wax between the base of your toilet and the floor flange. It seals the drain opening so wastewater and sewer gas go down the pipe and not onto the bathroom floor. When it fails, the toilet may look fine from the top and still be slowly soaking the floor beneath it.

Replacing a toilet wax ring is a job many homeowners can do with basic tools. It also hides a few problems that turn a one-hour fix into a bigger repair. This page covers both.

How to tell the seal has failed

Look at the base first. Water seeping out around the bottom of the bowl after a flush, even a few drops, suggests the seal is leaking. Wet caulk and a damp floor tile are early hints.

A sewer smell in the bathroom with no other source also points here, since the ring is also the gas barrier. A tight seal can still smell if a trap has dried out or a vent is blocked. A toilet that rocks or shifts when you sit on it can crush or break the seal because it moves against the wax with each use.

Not every puddle comes from the ring. Condensation on the tank, a loose supply line, or a leaking tank-to-bowl bolt all drip water that ends up on the floor. Dry everything, put a few sheets of toilet paper around the base, and see where they get wet after a flush.

What you need before you start

The tool list is short. You need a new wax ring or a wax-free seal, a pair of new closet bolts, a putty knife, a bucket and towels, rubber gloves, and a helper. A full toilet is heavy and awkward, so use two people, lift with your legs, and remember that dropping one cracks the bowl.

Wax rings are the traditional choice. They seal well on a flat flange at the right height. Wax-free seals, which are foam or rubber, are more forgiving of small height differences and can be repositioned. Either works if the flange is sound, and some people prefer the foam type because it does not smear on the floor.

Flange condition decides how it goes

The flange is the ring bolted to the floor around the drain opening. Once the toilet is off, look at it closely. It should be solid, flat, and attached to the floor, not cracked, bent, or rusted through.

Its height matters too. Ideally it sits at or slightly above the finished floor so the seal compresses properly. A flange sunk below the floor, often after new tile or flooring is laid, leaves a gap that a standard ring cannot fill. Spacers or a thicker seal can help, but a broken flange needs a proper repair.

A careful DIY swap, in outline

The general order is simple, and the details depend on your toilet. Shut off the water, flush to drain the tank, disconnect the supply line, remove the bolt caps and nuts, rock the toilet loose, and lift it straight up with a helper. Set it on towels away from the opening.

Scrape off the old wax, plug the drain opening with a rag to block sewer gas while you work, and pull it out before the toilet goes down. Inspect the flange and the floor, then set the new seal and lower the bowl straight down onto the bolts. Press down evenly, tighten the nuts a little at a time, and reconnect the water.

Flush several times, then check the base with a dry paper towel for seepage before you caulk or call it done.

Stop when you see any of this

Raw sewage on the floor, a broken flange, or soft flooring changes this from a quick fix to a repair that needs the right materials.

  • Cracked, bent, or rusted flange.
  • Soft, dark, or crumbling wood around the opening.
  • A toilet that still leaks after a fresh seal.
  • Standing wastewater that you cannot contain.

Wear gloves, wash up afterward, and keep children and pets out of the room while wastewater is on the floor. Wash hard surfaces with one household cleaner, and launder towels used on wastewater separately. Never mix cleaners, since bleach and ammonia-based products produce toxic fumes in a closed bathroom.

When a plumber should take over

A plumber checks things the average homeowner cannot easily see. That includes the condition of the drain pipe below the flange, whether the floor needs repair before the toilet goes back, and whether the flange can be fixed instead of replaced. See the toilet repair page for what that visit involves.

A seal is a small part. What drives the cost of a visit is a damaged flange, rotted subfloor, or a toilet that must be reset after floor work, so ask the plumber what the quote includes.

Toilet setting is plumbing code territory. Alabama's plumbing board rule 720-X-1-.02 names the 2021 International Residential Code for residential plumbing, and the board can tell you the current edition if you want to ask a plumber how a repair is done.

If several fixtures gurgle, back up, or smell at once, the problem may not be the ring at all. That points to a drain issue, and drain cleaning with a camera look is the better next step.
