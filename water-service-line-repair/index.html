Emergency Water Line Repair in Pelham, AL

The pipe between the street meter and your house is buried, out of sight, and carries everything you use. Here is how failures show up, who to call to find out who owns what, and how repair or replacement is decided.

If water is surfacing in the yard or your meter will not stop, close the main shutoff at the house, keep kids and pets off soft ground, and do not start digging. The service line is the buried pipe that carries water from the meter to your home, and repairing it usually means exposing a section or the whole run.

Emergency service line failures and what to do while you wait

A line that fails quickly looks dramatic. Water pools or bubbles up in the yard, pressure drops to a trickle, or the whole house loses flow. Close the house main, then open a faucet at the lowest point in the house to bleed off pressure. If water keeps surfacing in the yard after that, the break is likely ahead of your shutoff, so call us right away and say so.

Emergency dispatch covers these calls nights, weekends and holidays, with same-day appointments when available. Tell the dispatcher what you did with the main and whether the meter still moves. That helps the dispatcher understand what you are dealing with.

Where your responsibility starts and the utility's ends

Utilities differ on where their responsibility ends. Many stop at the meter, but Pelham Water Works sets its own rule, and this page cannot say where the line falls at your address. Call Pelham Water Works at (205) 620-6420 or visit 3111 Cummings St before any digging and ask which side the leak is on. If water is surfacing near the meter itself, or the leak is clearly before the meter, call them as well as the 24/7 line.

Helena's water utility is the Helena Utilities Board at (205) 663-1670, and the same ownership question applies. Calls from Helena come through the same 24/7 line.

How a failing line shows itself

  • A wet or sinking patch in the yard, even in dry weather
  • A drop in pressure at every tap, not just one
  • A meter that keeps moving with the house main closed
  • A bill that climbs for no clear reason
  • A hissing sound near the foundation or the meter box
  • Discolored water after a pressure surge

No single sign proves a service line problem. Low pressure can come from a partly closed valve or a failing regulator, and a wet patch can come from a sprinkler or a drain. A proper leak detection visit can use listening equipment and pressure testing to confirm the line before the yard is opened.

Patch it, replace it, dig it, or pull a new line through

A repair is a good fit when one fitting or one short section has failed and the rest of the pipe is sound. Replacement is the better bet when the line has failed more than once or the material is aging. A plumber looks at the failure point and the pipe next to it to judge.

Older service lines may be galvanized steel or copper, and newer ones are often plastic tubing, so the plumber identifies the material before choosing repair or replacement.

Low pressure that traces back to the buried line

If every fixture runs weak, the culprit may be the line that feeds the house. A partly collapsed pipe, a corroded section, or a slow leak can all rob pressure before it reaches your taps. Check one hose bib at the front and one fixture upstairs, since a strong outdoor flow but a weak indoor flow points elsewhere.

Test the main shutoff and any pressure regulator too, since these are smaller fixes. If pressure drops only when the irrigation runs, the issue is probably a sprinkler zone and not the service line.

Before anyone digs: 811, shutoffs and soft ground

Digging safety

Call 811 before any digging, even for a small shovel hole near the meter. Gas, electric and communication lines can share the same path.

  • Call 811 and wait for the lines to be marked
  • Find your house main and meter shutoff now
  • Open a tap in the house to confirm the water is off
  • Do not dig near a wet patch yourself. Wait for the locate and a plumber

Soft ground near a buried line has more than one possible cause in Shelby County. According to a 2014 Shelby County Reporter article, limestone bedrock underlies much of the county, and a sinkhole opened that year on Cambrian Ridge Drive in Pelham after heavy flooding. The Geological Survey of Alabama notes that limestone is susceptible to dissolution. A bowl-shaped depression, a depression that holds water, or one that keeps growing is a sign to stay clear.

If the depression is near a road or a structure, call the police, who alert Public Works. A plumber can tell you afterward whether your service line is involved. Do not stand over a growing hole to find out.
