Burst pipe emergency: the first ten minutes

  1. Find and close the main

    It is usually near the water heater, in a basement or crawlspace, or in a utility closet. If you cannot find it, or the house valve will not turn, do not force it. Call the 24/7 line and the dispatcher will talk you through it.

  2. Relieve pressure

    Open the lowest faucet and the highest one, then flush a toilet. The lines drain and the flow at the break slows.

  3. Cut power to the wet zone

    Use the breaker only if the panel is dry and you can reach it from dry footing. Otherwise stay away and tell the dispatcher.

  4. Save what you can

    Lift rugs and electronics off wet floors. Photograph the break, the water, and any damage before you clean.

  5. Call the 24/7 line

    Tell the dispatcher where the water is, whether the main is off, and whether the ceiling is sagging.

Weekend and overnight calls go through the same dispatch, and we book same-day appointments when available. If water is near the electrical panel or a ceiling is sagging with water, leave that room and call the 24/7 line from somewhere dry.

If you drained the lines, switch off the water heater at its breaker, from dry footing only, or at its gas control. If a pipe is frozen but not split, call the 24/7 line before doing anything else. Thaw it with warm air, never an open flame, and know where your main is in case it splits as it thaws.

Why a pipe lets go in the first place

Water expands when it freezes, and the ice can build pressure inside the pipe until the wall splits. That can happen when a hard freeze hits and a line runs through an unheated wall, attic, garage or crawlspace. The split often does not leak until the ice melts, which is why the flood can start hours after the cold ends.

Freezing is only one cause. High water pressure stresses fittings and old joints over time. Corrosion thins the wall of galvanized and some copper lines from the inside. Poor soldering, a loose compression nut, or a failed supply valve can also let go with no warning.

Copper, galvanized, PEX: how the material changes the repair

Copper splits along its length when frozen and can be repaired with a new section and fresh fittings. Galvanized steel is often rusty inside, so one burst may signal that neighboring sections are close behind. PEX is more flexible and may survive a freeze that splits copper, but a damaged or poorly crimped fitting can still fail.

A plumber will cut out the damaged stretch, check the pipe on either side, and decide whether a repair holds or a longer run should come out. If several joints in a line show corrosion, ask about repiping before the next failure. Alabama's Plumbers and Gas Fitters Examining Board rule 720-X-1-.02 names the 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) for residential plumbing, so check with the board for the current edition.

Supply valves, fittings and short broken stubs

A broken supply valve under a sink, a cracked angle stop behind a toilet, or a failed washing machine hose can flood a room just as fast. These can be simpler fixes if the shutoff upstream holds.

If the shutoff itself will not close, the main is the only way to stop the water. A plumber can replace a stuck valve once the line is isolated. A burst in the yard between the meter and the house is a different problem, covered under water service line repair.

After the water stops: photos, invoices and drying

Take photos of the broken pipe, the standing water, and every damaged surface before you start cleanup. Keep the plumber's invoice, since insurers and the water utility may ask for it. Restoration and drying are not plumbing services, so ask your insurer about water extraction and drying companies.

Wet drywall, insulation and carpet can grow mold if they stay damp, so start drying quickly. A plumber's job ends when the supply is repaired and tested, and the cleanup begins from there.
